# Interview about Awesome Life Science Resources

This is an English translation of an [interview originally published 13.04.2026 in German by *Laborjournal*](https://www.laborjournal.de/editorials/3470.php).

---

**You created Awesome Life Science Resources, a curated list of useful resources and tools for work culture, career development, and communication for life scientists. What motivated you to do this, and who is the list intended for?**

**JJF:** I think there are many things we need for our work in science that are not hard, technical skills. A lot surrounds our work—work culture, communication, career development, and so on. I often found myself returning to resources I had saved so that I could share them with colleagues. At some point, I thought it simply made sense to collect them all in one place and share them there directly. All the resources provide guidance on the broader, non-technical aspects of working in the life sciences, regardless of the specific research project. In that sense, they should be equally useful to PhD students, postdocs, and group leaders. I have received very positive feedback from all these groups, which also shows how little systematic guidance exists for some of these topics. Some resources address a healthy work culture, which I believe is often neglected. Others cover tasks that do not come up every day, but matter at very important moments—for example, when you need to communicate or publish your research findings effectively.

**The list must have taken a great deal of work. Can you give us an idea of how long you worked on it and how many entries it contains?**

**JJF:** In the end, it was less work than it might appear. During my PhD, I began bookmarking interesting resources I came across, and I have kept up that habit ever since—about eight years in total. Putting the collection into its current format took only a few days, spread over several weekends in my spare time. The list now contains 228 entries. However, I have already received the first new suggestions.

**You organized these 228 entries by topic, and the list also includes different types of resources. How did you approach this?**

**JJF:** The main sections are organized roughly in the order in which people typically move through stages of a scientific career: from entering a new work environment, through communicating their own work, to further career development. Most of the resources for each topic are articles, posts, and books, which are always sorted by reading time. For some topics, there are also videos, podcasts, tools, and collections comprising several articles or books. The format is based on the standardized “Awesome list” style commonly used on the code-hosting platform GitHub. To make the list a little clearer, however, I added emojis and approximate reading times.

**What do you mean by tools? Does that include AI tools, for example?**

**JJF:** By “tools,” I mean resources that you do not simply read, watch, or listen to, but rather things such as databases, templates, or software. As far as I know, there are no AI tools on the list yet. Since we are talking about AI tools: today's large language models can, of course, already offer useful advice on all these topics. But at the very least, this list will serve in the future as a record of what has proven valuable in practice. Incidentally, ChatGPT and Gemini were less helpful in creating the list than I had hoped; their descriptions were too general. AI is not yet good at understanding what is distinctive about a particular resource, and it was also unable to find new resources of sufficient quality. These tools were helpful only with reformatting.

**Do you use all the resources on the list yourself, or did you actively search for additional ones? Do you expand the list when you come across new resources?**

**JJF:** I have personally used most of these resources at least once in the past. I also actively searched for more, but unfortunately did not find many additional resources. I will definitely try to keep the list up to date.

**So people can send you suggestions?**

**JJF:** Yes. The list follows the usual “Awesome list” format on GitHub, which allows others to propose additions directly. I am, of course, also happy to receive suggestions via direct message. I would like to see the community and institutions create new resources—the need is definitely there!

**Could you recommend a few of your personal favorite resources?**

**JJF:** I would be happy to name a few personal favorites. The MIT [“Communication Kits”](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/broad/use-the-commkit/), with their useful and concise advice on how to communicate your science, are a highlight for me. Another resource I have often recommended is a [framework for regular conversations between supervisors and trainees](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2015.04.025) that deliberately puts mentoring and personal development at the center. There are also several resources on data visualization that I have read more than once because I am a very visual person - for example, a [blog post explaining why it is best to use black and white rather than colors when displaying a single microscopy channel](https://www.gabrielaplucinska.com/blog/2017/8/15/microscopic-images-1). There is also a nice [one-hour online book on how to give a talk](https://www.howtogiveatalk.com/). And, of course, there is the [timeless classic on how to write an abstract](https://www.nature.com/documents/nature-summary-paragraph.pdf).
